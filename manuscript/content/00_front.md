# Welcome to the Amish Pantry
* Walk into an Amish kitchen and you will almost always find a pantry lined with jars. Flour, oats, beans and sugar sit beside jars of mixes that were measured, blended and labeled on a quiet afternoon, ready to become biscuits, pancakes, soups and cakes on busy days. Nothing is wasted, very little is bought ready-made, and supper never depends on a last-minute trip to the store.
* This book brings that simple, thrifty way of cooking into your own home. You do not need special equipment, rare ingredients or years of baking experience. You need a few large bowls, a set of measuring cups, some clean jars and an hour or two to fill them.
### Why Make Your Own Mixes?
- Save money. A homemade mix costs a fraction of the boxed version, especially when you buy flour, sugar, oats and dry milk in bulk.
- Know what is in your food. Your mixes contain only the ingredients you put in them, with no preservatives, fillers or artificial flavors.
- Save time. Do the measuring once, then make from-scratch meals all month with only a few fresh ingredients.
- Be prepared. A well-stocked pantry means you can feed your family through snowstorms, busy seasons and tight weeks.
- Give from the heart. A layered jar of cookie or soup mix is one of the most welcome homemade gifts there is.

# How to Use This Book
* Every chapter in this book is built on one simple idea: one mix, many meals.
* Chapters 1 through 9 each open with five pantry mixes. Each mix recipe tells you what to combine, how much it makes, how to store it and how long it keeps. A short “How to Use” note gives you the basic method at a glance.
* After the mixes come ten everyday recipes made with them. At the top of each recipe you will see which mix it uses, so you can cook from whatever jars are already on your shelf. The One Mix, Many Meals Index at the back of the book lists every recipe by the mix it uses.

### Measuring Mixes the Right Way
- Stir or shake the mix before measuring, because heavier ingredients can settle.
- Spoon the mix lightly into a dry measuring cup and level it with the back of a knife. Never scoop straight from the jar or pack the cup, unless a recipe says so.
- Use standard U.S. measuring cups and spoons. A conversion chart is included at the back of the book.
### Symbols and Terms
- Makes: how much mix a recipe produces, or how many servings a recipe yields.
- Keeps: how long a mix stays at its best in the pantry, refrigerator or freezer.
- Uses: the pantry mix or mixes a recipe is made with.

# Pantry Staples & Ingredients
* Most of the mixes in this book are made from a short list of everyday ingredients. Buying these in larger bags is the easiest way to save money.
### Flours and Grains
- All-purpose flour is used in most mixes. Bread flour, with more protein, gives yeast breads a higher rise.
- Whole wheat flour and wheat bran add flavor and fiber but contain natural oils, so mixes made with them keep best in the refrigerator.
- Yellow cornmeal, old-fashioned rolled oats and quick-cooking oats are used for cornbread, oatmeal mixes, cookies and crisp toppings.
### Leaveners
- Baking powder and baking soda make quick breads, biscuits and pancakes rise. Both slowly lose strength, so check the date on the package.
- To test baking powder, stir 1 teaspoon into 1/2 cup hot water. It should bubble vigorously.
- Instant yeast is added to the bread mixes on baking day, not stored in the mix.
### Dry Dairy and Eggs
- Nonfat dry milk powder lets you add milk to a mix so that only water is needed later.
- Buttermilk powder gives pancakes, biscuits and ranch dressing their tangy flavor. Once opened, keep it in the refrigerator.
- Cheddar cheese powder is the base of the cheese sauce mix.
### Fats
- Vegetable shortening keeps much longer at room temperature than butter, which is why it is used in the all-purpose baking mixes.
- Mixes that need butter or oil have you add it fresh when you cook, so they keep longer on the shelf.
### Sugars, Cocoa and Flavorings
- Granulated sugar, brown sugar and powdered sugar.
- Unsweetened cocoa powder for cakes, brownies, puddings and drinks.
- Dried herbs and ground spices. Replace them once a year for the best flavor.
- Nutritional yeast, a savory, cheesy-tasting flake found in the baking or health-food aisle, is the base of the bouillon powders.

# Jars, Tools & Containers
### Containers
- Glass canning jars (pint, quart and half-gallon) with tight-fitting lids are ideal. They are airtight, easy to clean and let you see what is inside.
- Food-safe plastic bins with sealing lids work well for large batches of baking mix.
- Heavy-duty zip-top freezer bags are handy for single-batch portions such as cake, brownie and bread mixes.
- Oxygen absorbers can help long-term storage of dry, fat-free mixes such as beans, rice and soup mixes. Do not use them with mixes that contain fat.
### Tools
- A very large mixing bowl, a sturdy whisk and a pastry blender
- A wide-mouth canning funnel for filling jars
- Dry measuring cups and measuring spoons
- A blender, for powdering oats, bouillon and drink mixes
- Labels and a permanent marker
### Label Every Jar
* Every jar should show the name of the mix, the date it was made and the basic “How to Use” directions. Then anyone in the family can grab a jar and start cooking.

# Storing Mixes Safely
* Dry mixes are safe and long-lasting when they are kept clean, dry and cool. Follow these simple rules.
- Use clean, completely dry containers and utensils. Even a drop of water can cause clumping or mold.
- Store mixes in a cool, dry, dark place, away from the stove, dishwasher and sunny windows. Heat and humidity shorten shelf life.
- Mixes that contain fat (shortening), whole grains, buttermilk powder or cheese powder keep longest in the refrigerator or freezer. Check the “Keeps” line on each recipe.
- Let refrigerated or frozen mixes come to room temperature before opening the jar, so moisture does not condense inside.
- Always label your jars with the date. When in doubt, smell the mix: if it smells stale, sour or rancid, throw it away.
- Mixes that contain flour should be cooked before eating. Raw flour is not safe to eat, so do not taste unbaked dough or batter.
- Discard any coating mix that has touched raw meat. Shake only what you need into a separate bag.
